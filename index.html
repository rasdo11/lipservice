The Injection Report
The Under-Eye Filler Depth Problem Just Got Quantified

What's going on: A study published this week in *Aesthetic Surgery Journal* used high-frequency ultrasound to map HA filler placement in 94 patients reporting bluish discoloration (Tyndall effect) under the eyes. In 78% of cases, filler was placed in the superficial subcutaneous plane — too shallow for the tear trough by 1.5–2mm on average. The authors call it a "systematic depth error" tied to providers using needle-based technique instead of cannula at the supraperiosteal level.

What it means: Tyndall effect isn't bad filler — it's filler in the wrong plane. The study confirms that deep, on-bone placement with a cannula virtually eliminates that blue-gray cast. If your under-eyes look vaguely bruised months after treatment, it's not your skin being "translucent." It's product sitting where light can scatter through it.

What's next: Ask your injector one question before your next tear trough appointment: "What plane are you placing in?" The answer should be supraperiosteal. If they say subdermal or don't specify, that's your cue.

The question to ask before under-eye filler: "What depth are you placing at?" The only right answer for tear troughs is supraperiosteal — on the bone.

Put It In Your Mouth
High-Dose Cocoa Flavanols Just Got a Cardiology Co-Sign

What's going on: Follow-up data from the COSMOS trial, published this week in *The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, showed that 500mg/day of cocoa flavanols reduced cardiovascular events by 27% in adults over 60 — and the effect was strongest in participants with the lowest baseline fruit and vegetable intake. This is the largest flavanol supplementation trial ever completed.

What that means: The dose matters and you're not getting it from a Dove bar. 500mg of cocoa flavanols is roughly 10 tablespoons of natural cocoa powder. Supplementation (CocoaVia is the brand used in the trial) is the realistic path. The skin benefit is secondary but real — flavanols improve dermal blood flow, which shows up as that "I just worked out" flush without the workout.

This week's cheat meal: A double-thick slice of flourless chocolate torte from wherever does it best near you. Dense, fudgy, zero pretense. Pair with espresso.

What to cut: Those "beauty chocolates" with 30mg of flavanols per piece. That's a marketing dose, not a clinical one.

Lip Lab
Glossier Quietly Reformulated Ultralip and Nobody's Talking About It

What's going on: Glossier updated the Ultralip formula this month — the ingredient list on their site now shows hydrogenated polyisobutene replacing the previous isododecane base, and they've added squalane. No announcement, no relaunch fanfare. Reddit caught it.

Why it matters: Isododecane is a volatile solvent that evaporates fast — it's why the original Ultralip felt weightless but dried some people out within an hour. Hydrogenated polyisobutene is a long-wear emollient that stays put. Combined with squalane, this is a fundamentally different wear experience: heavier on application, but the slip lasts.

The verdict: If you ditched Ultralip for being too fleeting, revisit. If you loved the barely-there weight, you might find the new formula too balmy. Cachet and Vesper are the shades to test — they show the texture shift most clearly.

Glossier swapped Ultralip's fast-evaporating solvent base for a long-wear emollient. Same name, different formula. Worth retesting if you bounced.
