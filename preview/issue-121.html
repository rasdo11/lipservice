The Injection Report
High-Dose Botox Is Buying You More Time Than You Think

What's going on: A study published this week in *Aesthetic Surgery Journal* found that patients receiving 40–60 units of onabotulinumtoxinA in the upper face maintained clinical effect for a median of 5.8 months — nearly double the traditional 3-month rebooking window most practices default to. The cohort was 120 women, ages 30–55, tracked over 18 months with standardized photography and patient-reported outcomes.

What it means: This isn't about pumping in more product for fun. Higher initial doses appear to extend duration without increasing adverse events, which means fewer appointments and potentially lower annual spend. The catch: dosing decisions are injector-dependent, and most practices still default to conservative units because insurance logic trained everyone that way.

What's next: If you're rebooking every 12 weeks like clockwork, ask your injector whether a higher initial dose with longer intervals has been on their radar. Bring the study. They've probably already read it.

The question to ask at your next appointment: 'Based on my muscle mass and metabolism, am I a candidate for a higher-dose, longer-interval protocol?' Let them do the math.

Put It In Your Mouth
Watermelon Just Got a Clinical Glow-Up

What's going on: Researchers at the University of Naples published findings this week in *Nutrients* showing that L-citrulline — the amino acid watermelon is loaded with — significantly improved skin microcirculation in a 4-week supplementation trial. Participants showed measurable increases in dermal blood flow and self-reported improvements in skin 'radiance,' which the researchers actually managed to quantify with laser Doppler imaging.

What that means: Citrulline converts to arginine, which drives nitric oxide production, which dilates blood vessels. More blood flow to the dermis means better nutrient delivery and that flushed-from-within look no highlighter can fake. Two cups of watermelon gives you roughly 1.5g of citrulline — the study used 2g daily.

Lip Lab
Glossier Quietly Changed Ultralip and Hoped You Wouldn't Notice

What's going on: Eagle-eyed Redditors on r/glossier flagged this week that Ultralip's ingredient list has changed — castor oil moved up, the meadowfoam seed oil is gone entirely, and a new silicone crosspolymer appeared. Glossier hasn't announced a reformulation.

Why it matters: Meadowfoam seed oil was arguably what made Ultralip feel different from every other sheer balm-lipstick hybrid — it's a high-stability emollient that plays well with lip skin. Replacing it with a silicone changes the slip, the wear, and the moisture mechanism. Early reports say the new version is shinier but dries down faster and feels thinner.

The verdict: If you stockpiled the old formula, you were accidentally smart. The new version is fine — good color payoff, comfortable — but it's now competing in a much more crowded lane. It lost the thing that made it *it*.

Check your tube's batch code on checkcosmetic.net — if it was manufactured before March 2025, you have the original formula. Guard it accordingly.
