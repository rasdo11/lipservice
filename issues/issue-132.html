The Injection Report
The Ozempic Face Deficit Has a Number Now

What's going on: A retrospective study published this week in *Aesthetic Surgery Journal* analyzed 3D facial imaging of 112 patients on GLP-1 agonists and found an average 2.4 mL of midface volume loss per 15 lbs of body weight lost — roughly double what clinicians had been estimating clinically. The loss concentrates in the deep medial cheek fat pad and nasolabial region, accelerating after the 25-lb mark.

What it means: This isn't just "you look gaunt." The volume loss pattern mimics aging by 8–10 years, per the study authors, because it hits the same fat compartments that deflate in your 50s and 60s. Injectors are already shifting protocols — expect to hear "prejuvenation filler" pitched before or alongside GLP-1 prescriptions.

What's next: If you're on semaglutide or tirzepatide and past the 20-lb mark, get a baseline 3D scan now. Ask your injector about staged volumization rather than one corrective session after the weight is gone.

The magic question for your injector: "Are you treating my current face or planning for where the volume loss is heading?" Staged beats reactive every time.

Put It In Your Mouth
Sardines Just Got a Clinical Cosign

What's going on: A study out of the University of Barcelona published Monday in *Clinical Nutrition* found that participants who ate two servings of sardines per week for 12 months showed significantly improved skin elasticity scores and reduced inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) compared to controls — even after adjusting for overall omega-3 intake. The researchers credit the combination of omega-3s, vitamin D, and the calcium from edible bones working synergistically.

What that means: Your collagen supplement is shaking. Whole sardines appear to deliver a matrix of skin-relevant nutrients that isolated supplements can't replicate. The anti-inflammatory effect was dose-dependent and showed up at the skin level by month four.

Lip Lab
Your Lip Oil's "Hyaluronic Acid" Might Be Polyglutamic Acid

What's going on: Cosmetic chemist Javon Ford went viral this week after HPLC-testing five bestselling lip oils claiming hyaluronic acid on the front label — three contained polyglutamic acid instead, which is cheaper and has a different moisture-retention mechanism. He named names: two drugstore, one prestige.

Why it matters: PGA isn't bad — it actually forms a better film on lips. But it's not HA, and labeling it as such is, at best, misleading. The FDA's cosmetic labeling rules require INCI accuracy on the back panel, but front-of-pack marketing claims operate in a gray zone that rarely gets enforced.

The verdict: Check your INCI list. If you see "polyglutamic acid" and the front says "hyaluronic," the product works fine but the brand just told you who they are.

Flip the tube. The INCI list on the back is legally required to be accurate. The front label is marketing. They are not the same document.
